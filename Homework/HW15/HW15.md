1. ## Explain monolithic architecture, service oriented architecture and Micro service architecture.

**At a glance**

| **Architecture**                        | **Core idea**                                                | **Typical when…**                                            | **Key trade-offs**                                           |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Monolithic**                          | **All application logic is packaged & deployed as one process/binary** | - Team is small- Domain is simple- Latency between modules must be near-zero | ✅ Simple to develop, test & deploy❌ Hard to scale parts independently; any change → full redeploy |
| **Service-Oriented Architecture (SOA)** | **Coarse-grained business “services” communicate over an enterprise bus** | - Multiple heterogeneous systems must interoperate- You need strong governance (e.g., ESB, WS-* standards) | ✅ Reuse existing systems; central policy enforcement❌ ESB becomes a bottleneck; heavy XML/WS-* overhead |
| **Microservices**                       | **Each bounded context runs as an independently deployable service with its own data store** | - Domain is large & evolving- You need autonomous, DevOps-style teams | ✅ Granular scaling & release cadence; fault isolation❌ Distributed-systems complexity (network, consistency, observability) |

------

### **1 Monolithic Architecture**

- **Structure** – UI, business logic, and data-access layers compile into **one deployable unit** (e.g., a single JAR/WAR, Docker container, or binary).
- **Strengths**
  - **Simplicity** (单体 *Monolith*): one codebase, one set of dependencies.
  - **Performance** – in-process calls, no network latency.
  - **Transactional integrity** – single relational database is easy to ACID-protect.
- **Limitations** (boundary conditions)
  - **Scalability hot-spots** – you must scale the whole app, even if only one module is CPU-bound.
  - **Release risk** – small change → full redeploy (长时间停机 *downtime* if not automated).
  - **Team coupling** – large codebase leads to merge conflicts and fragile ownership.
- **Edge cases** – “modular monolith” uses internal modules to delay the jump to services; works until organizational or scaling pressure grows.

------

### 2 Service-Oriented Architecture (SOA)

- **Concept** – Business capabilities are exposed as **shared enterprise services** (often via SOAP + WSDL) and integrated through an **Enterprise Service Bus (ESB)** that handles routing, transformation, security, and orchestration.
- **Typical stack** – ESB (e.g., Mule, IBM Integration Bus), XML, WS-Security, XSLT transforms, centralized governance registry.
- **Advantages**
  - **System integration** – wrap legacy ERP/CRM systems behind services.
  - **Governance & policy enforcement** – ESB can enforce SLAs, logging, security.
- **Pitfalls**
  - **ESB as a single point of failure** (and performance choke point).
  - **High ceremony** – verbose XML contracts, strict change control slow teams.
  - **“Mini-monolith” services** – coarse, often share databases → still coupled.
- **When it shines** – large enterprises needing to surface existing systems without a full rewrite.

------

### 3 Microservice Architecture

- **Definition** – **Small, independently deployable services** built around **bounded contexts** (DDD). Each service owns its **data**; interactions are over lightweight protocols (REST/gRPC, messaging).
- **Principles**
  - **Single Responsibility** – one business capability per service.
  - **Autonomous teams** – “you build it, you run it” DevOps.
  - **Decentralized data & governance** – polyglot persistence, version-tolerant APIs.
- **Benefits**
  - **Elastic scaling** – hotspot service scales horizontally without touching others.
  - **Continuous delivery** – services release on their own cadence.
  - **Fault isolation** – failure stays within service (if circuit breakers, retries, bulkheads are in place).
- **Challenges / Edge cases**
  - **Distributed complexity** – network latency, partial failures, eventual consistency (CAP theorem).
  - **Operational overhead** – CI/CD pipelines, container orchestration (Kubernetes), observability stack (logging, metrics, tracing) for **every** service.
  - **Data consistency** – need patterns like **saga**, **event sourcing**, or **CQRS** for cross-service transactions.
- **When to adopt** – multiple agile teams, high scale or change velocity, willingness to invest in platform/DevOps maturity.

------

### **Choosing among them**

1. **Start simple** – A well-structured modular monolith is easiest to operate for a small team or early-stage product.
2. **Move to microservices incrementally** once organizational or scaling needs justify the added complexity. A common path is **“strangler pattern”** – peel off one bounded context at a time.
3. **SOA vs. microservices** – SOA focuses on enterprise integration & governance; microservices emphasize team autonomy and CI/CD. Modern microservice ecosystems often use an **API Gateway + event mesh**, avoiding the heavyweight ESB of classical SOA.

------

**Rule of thumb**

> **Optimize for the constraint you actually face**: if deployment frequency and team autonomy are bottlenecks → microservices; if legacy integration is hardest → SOA patterns; if neither, a modular monolith may be all you need.

2. ## Document the microservice architeture and core components

   ## Core Idea

   Build **independently deployable, loosely coupled** services around **bounded contexts** so that small teams can own, release, and scale their part of the system without coordinating large‑scale deployments.

   ------

   ## High‑Level Component Map

   ```
   [ Client ] → (API Gateway) → { Service A | Service B | Service C } ↔ (Service Mesh) ↔ (Databases) ↔ (Message Broker)
                                  ↑
                      (Service Registry / Discovery)
                                  ↓
                      (Config Server) & (Secrets Store)
   
          ⤴ Observability Stack (Logs, Metrics, Traces)
          ⤴ CI/CD Pipeline  ⤴ Security / Identity Provider
   ```

   *Dashed arrows indicate cross‑cutting concerns such as tracing, metrics, and security tokens injected by the service mesh.*

   ------

   ## Core Components

   | #    | Component                               | Responsibility                                               | Typical Tech                                 | Edge Cases / Notes                                           |
   | ---- | --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
   | 1    | **Microservice**                        | Owns a single business capability & its **data schema**; exposes API/Events | Spring Boot, Express.js, Go, .NET            | Avoid **God‑class** services; respect bounded context to prevent mini‑monoliths |
   | 2    | **API Gateway**                         | Single entrypoint: routing, rate‑limiting, auth, aggregation | Kong, APIGW, NGINX, AWS API Gateway          | Becomes a choke point; use blue‑green rollouts to update rules safely |
   | 3    | **Service Registry / Discovery**        | Keeps track of service instances and health; enables dynamic client‑side or server‑side load‑balancing | Eureka, Consul, Kubernetes DNS               | Network partitions can cause stale registry; add TTLs & health probes |
   | 4    | **Configuration Server**                | Centralized dynamic config; supports **immutable infrastructure** principle | Spring Cloud Config, etcd, Vault             | Hot‑reloading risks inconsistent state—version configs & roll back |
   | 5    | **Service Mesh (Data + Control Plane)** | Transparent **L4/L7** traffic management, mTLS, retries, circuit breaking | Istio, Linkerd, AWS App Mesh                 | Adds latency (<2 ms) & resource overhead; consider only when scale >~10 services |
   | 6    | **Database per Service**                | Strong schema isolation; allows polyglot persistence         | PostgreSQL, MongoDB, DynamoDB                | Cross‑service joins impossible—solve with Sagas / CQRS / Denormalization |
   | 7    | **Event Bus / Message Broker**          | Async decoupling, eventual consistency, back‑pressure handling | Kafka, RabbitMQ, SNS/SQS                     | Configure idempotent consumers; handle out‑of‑order delivery |
   | 8    | **Observability Stack**                 | Central **logging**, **metrics**, and **tracing** for troubleshooting & SLOs | ELK/EFK, Prometheus + Grafana, OpenTelemetry | High‑cardinality metrics explode storage; sample traces smartly |
   | 9    | **Resilience Layer**                    | Circuit breakers, bulkheads, rate limiters                   | Resilience4j, Hystrix, Envoy filters         | Too‑aggressive trips cause cascading failures; tune thresholds |
   | 10   | **Security / Identity Provider**        | AuthN/AuthZ, token issuance (OAuth 2.0/JWT), secrets storage | Keycloak, Auth0, Cognito, Vault              | Clock‑skew breaks JWT validation; use NTP & short TTL refresh tokens |
   | 11   | **CI/CD Pipeline**                      | Automated build, test, container image publish, progressive delivery | GitHub Actions, Jenkins X, Argo CD           | Dependency hell across services—enforce semantic versioning  |
   | 12   | **Container Orchestrator**              | Schedules service containers, manages scaling & self‑healing | Kubernetes, ECS, Nomad                       | YAML sprawl; use Helm/Kustomize or GitOps to manage manifests |

   ------

   ## Interaction Flow (Request–Response)

   1. **Client** sends HTTPS request with JWT → **API Gateway**.
   2. Gateway authenticates, enforces quotas, forwards to **Service A**.
   3. **Service A** queries its DB; needs data owned by **Service B** ⇒ emits query on **Event Bus** or makes REST/gRPC call discovered via **Service Registry**.
   4. **Service Mesh** sidecars add tracing headers, enforce retries, and encrypt traffic.
   5. Both services push structured logs/metrics to **Observability Stack**.
   6. Pipeline monitors SLOs; alerts on PagerDuty if error‑rate > threshold.

   ------

   ## Cross‑Cutting Patterns

   - **Saga Pattern** for distributed transactions
   - **CQRS & Event Sourcing** for read/write separation
   - **Blue‑Green / Canary** deployments via progressive rollout controller
   - **Zero‑Trust Security** with mutual TLS and least‑privilege tokens

   ------

   ## Pros & Cons Snapshot

   | ✅ Strengths                            | ❌ Challenges                              |
   | -------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
   | Granular scaling & rapid releases      | Operational overhead (K8s, observability) |
   | Fault isolation (blast‑radius control) | Distributed data consistency              |
   | Polyglot tech & data stores            | Debugging across many hops                |
   | Team autonomy (Conway’s Law alignment) | Requires DevOps/platform maturity         |

   ------

   ## Reference Checklist for Production Readiness

   -  Health checks & graceful shutdown
   -  Backward‑compatible API versioning policy
   -  Centralized structured logging with trace IDs
   -  Circuit breakers & timeouts around all remote calls
   -  Automated, reproducible builds & rollbacks
   -  Secrets managed via encrypted vault & rotated
   -  SLOs with alerting on latency, errors, saturation (“RED” & “USE” metrics)
   -  Disaster recovery runbook & regular fire drills

   ------

   > **Rule of Thumb** Start with a **modular monolith**. Introduce these components incrementally as **scale, team autonomy, or reliability needs** justify the complexity.

3. ## Explain Resilience patterns? Explain circuit breaker with Spring Cloud Hystrix code example.

   ### **1 Key Resilience Patterns in Distributed Systems**

   | **Pattern**                         | **Purpose**                                                  | **Typical “When to Use”**                                    | **Watch-outs / Edge cases**                                  |
   | ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
   | **Timeout**                         | Abort a slow remote call so resources are not blocked indefinitely. | Any call that crosses a process boundary.                    | If timeout < normal latency P95 ⇒ false failures; if too large ⇒ threads exhausted. |
   | **Retry**                           | Transparently re-attempt a transient failure.                | Network glitches, 5xx from stateless service, idempotent ops. | Exponential back-off + jitter to avoid *retry storms*; never retry non-idempotent writes. |
   | **Circuit Breaker**                 | Stop sending traffic to an unhealthy dependency after error threshold is exceeded, then periodically probe to see if it’s healthy. | Downstream has > X% errors/latency spikes.                   | Poor tuning can trip during brief spikes; half-open window must be small to prevent avalanche. |
   | **Bulkhead / Isolation**            | Allocate separate thread/connection pools so one dependency can’t exhaust all resources. | Services that serve vastly different workloads (e.g., search vs. bulk export). | Starving low-traffic pool leads to false downtime—monitor pool saturation. |
   | **Fallback / Graceful Degradation** | Return a default or cached response instead of failing the whole user request. | Non-critical enrichment data (ratings, recommendations).     | Stale data may mislead users; log when fallback is used for SRE visibility. |
   | **Rate Limiter / Throttling**       | Keep load within capacity limits; protect from abusive clients. | Public APIs, login endpoints.                                | Coordinate limiter across replicas to avoid uneven enforcement. |
   | **Load Shedding / Queue Reject**    | Proactively reject excess work when CPU/threads saturated to keep latency bounded. | High-traffic flash sales, DDoS.                              | Combine with graceful error message; watch for feedback loops. |
   | **Cache-Aside & Idempotency Keys**  | Reduce repeated expensive calls; dedupe duplicate requests.  | Hot reads, payment retries.                                  | Ensure cache eviction + write-through policies to prevent stale/dirty reads. |

   > **Rule of thumb:** Combine *timeout → retry → circuit breaker → bulkhead* in this order; add rate-limiting and fallback where business value allows.

   ------

   ### **2 Circuit Breaker Deep-Dive**

   | **State**     | **Description**                              | **Transition Trigger**                             |
   | ------------- | -------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
   | **Closed**    | Calls flow as normal; errors are counted.    | Error rate > threshold *within* rolling window.    |
   | **Open**      | Calls fail fast (or instantly use fallback). | After sleepWindow, switch to half-open.            |
   | **Half-open** | Allow limited “probe” traffic.               | **Successes** → Closed; **failures** → Open again. |

   This prevents *retry storms* and protects both caller and callee.

   ------

   ### **3 Spring Cloud Netflix Hystrix Code Example**

   > *Full-line comments illustrate what each line does (user preference).*

   ```java
   // ProductService.java
   package com.example.catalog.service;
   
   import com.netflix.hystrix.contrib.javanica.annotation.HystrixCommand;      // Hystrix AOP annotation
   import com.netflix.hystrix.contrib.javanica.annotation.HystrixProperty;     // Tune breaker properties
   import org.springframework.beans.factory.annotation.Autowired;
   import org.springframework.stereotype.Service;
   import org.springframework.web.client.RestTemplate;
   
   @Service                                                                   // Marks this class as a Spring bean
   public class ProductService {
   
       @Autowired                                                             // Inject client to call inventory microservice
       private RestTemplate restTemplate;
   
       // Primary method wrapped by Hystrix ----------------------------------
       @HystrixCommand(
           fallbackMethod = "getDefaultProduct",                              // Fallback executed when breaker is OPEN or call fails
           commandProperties = {                                              // Runtime tuning parameters
               @HystrixProperty(name = "execution.isolation.thread.timeoutInMilliseconds", value = "2000"),  // 2-s timeout
               @HystrixProperty(name = "circuitBreaker.requestVolumeThreshold", value = "5"),                 // Min 5 requests in window
               @HystrixProperty(name = "circuitBreaker.errorThresholdPercentage", value = "50"),              // Trip if ≥50 % fail
               @HystrixProperty(name = "circuitBreaker.sleepWindowInMilliseconds", value = "5000")            // Stay OPEN for 5 s
           }
       )
       public Product getProductById(Long id) {                              // Business method – fetch product stock info
           // Remote call: logically may throw RestClientException / timeout
           return restTemplate.getForObject(
               "http://inventory-service/api/products/" + id,                // Service discovered via Ribbon/K8s DNS
               Product.class);
       }
   
       // ----------- Fallback ------------
       public Product getDefaultProduct(Long id) {                           // Executed when breaker OPEN or timeout/error
           return new Product(id, "N/A", "Product information currently unavailable");  // Safe default response
       }
   }
   ```

   **Supporting beans**

   ```java
   // Application.java – configure RestTemplate with Ribbon LB + timeout
   @Bean
   @LoadBalanced                                  // Enables client-side load-balancing & discovery
   public RestTemplate restTemplate() {
       SimpleClientHttpRequestFactory factory = new SimpleClientHttpRequestFactory();
       factory.setConnectTimeout(500);            // 0.5 s connect timeout
       factory.setReadTimeout(1500);              // 1.5 s read timeout
       return new RestTemplate(factory);
   }
   ```

   **How it works**

   1. Each call to getProductById passes through Hystrix’s proxy.
   2. If ≥ 50 % of the last **5** requests fail or take > 2 s, the circuit trips to **OPEN**.
   3. Calls now short-circuit to getDefaultProduct (no network hop) for **5 s**.
   4. After the sleep window, Hystrix moves to **HALF-OPEN** and lets **one** probe call through:
      - Success → metrics reset, state → **CLOSED**.
      - Failure → state → **OPEN** for another 5 s.

   **Operational Tips**

   - **Thread vs. semaphore isolation:** default is thread pool; size it (e.g., coreSize=10) to avoid starvation.
   - **Monitor** Hystrix streams (Turbine + Hystrix Dashboard) or migrate to Resilience4j since Hystrix is end-of-life.
   - **Combine** with bulkhead (dedicated thread pool) and retries (outside the breaker) for complete resilience stack.

   ------

   ### **4 Boundary Conditions & Best Practices**

   - **Short-lived spikes**: Raise requestVolumeThreshold so the breaker ignores tiny blips.
   - **Cascading failures**: Place circuit breakers on every remote hop, not just at the edge.
   - **Idempotency**: If you add automatic retries outside Hystrix, ensure the operation is safe to repeat.
   - **Graceful Degradation UX**: Communicate fallback data to users (e.g., “Inventory is temporarily unavailable”).

   > **Remember:** Tune with real production latency/error metrics and load-test failure scenarios (chaos engineering) to validate that timeouts, retries, and circuit breakers interact as intended.

4. ## Explain load balancing algorithms.

   ### **Big picture — What a load-balancing algorithm decides**

   “Which healthy backend instance should handle the next request?” Good algorithms maximise **throughput**, keep **latency** predictable, and avoid over- or under-utilising individual nodes.

   ------

   ## **1 Static / deterministic algorithms**

   Require no runtime feedback from servers.

   | **Algorithm**                                 | **How it works**                                             | **When it shines**                                           | **Caveats**                                                  |
   | --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
   | **Round-Robin**                               | Cycles through the server list in order.                     | Homogeneous servers; sessions are *stateless*.               | Ignores load differences; busy node may still get new work.  |
   | **Weighted Round-Robin**                      | Each server is given a weight (capacity factor); entries are repeated proportionally in the schedule. | Pool with predictable capacity differences (32 CPU vs 8 CPU). | Weights are static ⇒ can drift from reality if traffic mix changes. |
   | **Source-IP Hash / URL Hash**                 | Deterministically maps client IP (or URL, user ID) to a server via a hash function. | **Session stickiness** without cookies; cache farms (CDNs).  | Uneven hash → hot spots; pool change causes massive remap unless you use *consistent hashing*. |
   | **Consistent Hashing (e.g., Ketama, Maglev)** | Hash ring; only buckets near the removed/added node are remapped. | Distributed caches, sharded key-value stores.                | Harder to implement; still needs virtual‐nodes tuning to smooth load. |

   ------

   ## **2 Dynamic / feedback-driven algorithms**

   Use real-time metrics (connection count, latency, CPU, EWMA², etc.).

   | **Algorithm**                                            | **Runtime signal**                                           | **Key strength**                                             | **Edge cases & tuning tips**                                 |
   | -------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
   | **Least Connections**                                    | Active connections                                           | Great for long-lived connections (WebSockets, DB).           | Bursty short-lived traffic looks “light”—can overload slow closers. |
   | **Least Response Time / Least Latency**                  | Rolling average latency                                      | Directly targets user experience.                            | Needs stable, low-jitter measurements; noisy links can mislead. |
   | **Weighted Least Connections / Latency**                 | Signal × weight                                              | Handles hetero capacity *and* live load.                     | Pick accurate weights; re-weigh periodically.                |
   | **Peak Exponentially-Weighted Moving Average (PEX)**     | EWMA of queue/latency (Envoy, NGINX “least_time”)            | Smooths spikes; adapts quickly without oscillation.          | Still affected by coordinated mutations (e.g., GC pauses) unless jitter added. |
   | **Random-two-choices**                                   | Pick 2 targets at random, choose the “better” (fewer conns/latency) | ➊ Near-optimal load balance ➋ O(1) stats                     | Needs only *sampling* rather than global stats; widely used in big L4/L7 proxies. |
   | **Adaptive Request Routing (Ring-hash + token buckets)** | Success rate + latency histograms                            | Shifts traffic away from error-prone pods fast (gRPC LB, Istio). | More complex; relies on good failure signalling.             |

   > **EWMA²** (Exponentially Weighted Moving Average of latency²) heavily penalises spikes, keeping tail-latency low.

   ------

   ## **3 Specialised strategies**

   | **Name**                                      | **Purpose**                                          | **Key ideas**                                                |
   | --------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
   | **Geographic / Topology-aware**               | Send client to nearest region or AZ to minimise RTT. | Geo-DNS, Anycast BGP, topology hints in Kubernetes.          |
   | **Layer-7 Content-based**                     | Route by URL path, HTTP header, or gRPC method.      | API gateways, canary releases (/v2/** → new pool).           |
   | **Server-driven / Client-side LB**            | Client picks target from a service-discovery list.   | gRPC pick-first, xDS, micro-service meshes; reduces hop, but each client must run algorithm & health-checks. |
   | **Queue-length estimation (SRE “load shed”)** | Route only if predicted queue < threshold; else 429. | Google’s “Global LB SEV” paper; protects tail latency.       |

   ------

   ## **4 Choosing the right algorithm**

   1. **Stateless, uniform pool** → *Round-Robin* is often “good enough”.
   2. **Differently sized nodes** → *Weighted* variants.
   3. **Long-lived connections** → *Least Connections*.
   4. **User-affinity / caching** → *IP-hash* or *consistent hashing*.
   5. **Highly variable latency** → *EWMA Latency* or *random-two-choices*.
   6. **Edge/CDN or multi-region** → *Geo-routing* + local LB per region.

   Always pair the algorithm with **health checks**, **circuit-breakers**, and—if using feedback—**observation windows** large enough to avoid flapping but small enough to adapt quickly.

   ------

   ### **Practical tuning checklist**

   - **Timeouts < p99 latency** or breaker trips will shift *all* traffic suddenly.
   - Use **slow-start**: newly recovered nodes ramp traffic gradually.
   - **Metrics accuracy** matters more than fancy maths—instrument queue depth, success rate, and latency at the proxy.
   - In cloud auto-scaling groups, align LB weights with instance vCPU or *target-tracking* policies to avoid oversubscription.

   > **Rule of thumb:** start simple, measure, then evolve. A well-instrumented *Round-Robin + slow-start + health checks* often beats an untuned “smart” algorithm.

5. ## Explain API Gateway.

   Single reverse-proxy front door that **routes, secures, throttles, and shapes** traffic for all back-end services. It terminates TLS, validates JWT/OAuth, applies rate limits/circuit-breakers, aggregates or transforms requests, and emits uniform logs/traces—letting microservices stay focused on business logic while clients see one stable entry-point.

6. ## Explain service discovery and service registry

   **Service Registry vs. Service Discovery—quick primer**

   | **Concept**           | **What it is**                                               | **Who uses it**                                              | **Common tech**                                        |
   | --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ |
   | **Service Registry**  | A **database of live service instances** (name → IP:port, metadata, health status). | Discovery clients and load balancers query or watch it.      | Eureka, Consul, etcd, ZooKeeper, Kubernetes API server |
   | **Service Discovery** | The **process** of resolving a logical service name to a healthy instance. Implements lookup, caching, and (optionally) client-side load-balancing. | Can run **in clients** (library/sidecar) or **at a proxy** (API-gateway / ingress LB). | Ribbon/Feign, Spring Cloud, Envoy/xDS, CoreDNS         |

   ------

   ### **How it works (dynamic model)**

   1. **Registration** – On startup, each service instance self-registers with the registry and begins sending heartbeat or health-check responses.
   2. **Lookup** – Caller requests inventory-service → discovery mechanism queries local cache or the registry for healthy endpoints.
   3. **Load-balance** – Either the client library (client-side LB) or an external proxy (server-side LB) picks an instance using round-robin/least-connections/etc.
   4. **Deregistration** – On graceful shutdown or failed health checks the instance is removed, preventing traffic to a dead pod/VM.

   ------

   ### **Two discovery patterns**

   | **Pattern**                                          | **Flow**                                                | **Pros**                                              | **Cons**                                                     |
   | ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
   | **Client-side** (Netflix OSS, gRPC LB, K8s headless) | Client ↔ Registry (GET) → choose instance → direct call | No extra hop; smart fail-over                         | Every client needs discovery code; harder to enforce cross-cutting policies |
   | **Server-side** (AWS ALB, Istio Ingress)             | Client → Proxy/LB ↔ Registry → proxy picks instance     | Central place for auth, rate-limit, TLS; thin clients | Extra network hop; proxy can become bottleneck               |

   ------

   ### **Edge-case notes**

   - **Stale cache** – use TTLs + periodic delta updates to avoid routing to terminated pods.
   - **Split-brain** – ensure registries replicate or raft-elect a leader to avoid inconsistent views.
   - **Startup race** – don’t send traffic until health probe passes *and* registration confirmed (readiness probe in K8s).
   - **Service mesh** – sidecar (Envoy) handles discovery via control-plane (Istio xDS) → combines client-side smarts with proxy enforcement.

   > **Rule of thumb:** small clusters can use simple DNS-based discovery; as services grow (>-dozens) adopt a dedicated registry plus either client- or proxy-based discovery to keep traffic reliable.
